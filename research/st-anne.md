# Historical and Theological Context: St Anne — Grief, Bereavement, and the Shadow of the Passion

## Who is St Anne? — The Source Problem

All information about the names and lives of Joachim and Anne comes from apocryphal literature.
The three principal sources are:

- **Protevangelium of James** (c. AD 150) — the earliest and most influential account
- **Gospel of the Nativity of Mary** (3rd century)
- **Gospel of Pseudo-Matthew** — a Latin retelling that popularised the stories in the West

Her authority was contested: in the West the *Protevangelium* was condemned by Jerome,
Pope Damasus I, Pope Innocent I, and Pope Gelasius I — yet its stories spread regardless,
eventually reaching the Western church through Jacobus de Voragine's *Golden Legend* (13th century).

**Name:** Anne derives from the Hebrew *Ḥannah*, meaning "grace."

## Anne's Life Narrative (from Apocryphal Sources)

- Born in Bethlehem, Judaea; married Joachim; lived a devout life in Nazareth
- Long childlessness — her barrenness was a source of shame and grief
- Her story closely parallels the Old Testament Hannah (1 Samuel 1), who also prayed
  desperately for a child and dedicated that child to God's service
- Joachim was publicly humiliated at the Temple and retreated to the wilderness to pray
- Anne wept alone, praying for God to remove the "curse of sterility"
- Both received separate angelic visitations promising a miraculous child
- Mary was born; at age three, the couple dedicated her to service in the Temple in Jerusalem
- This act of consecration — surrendering a beloved daughter to God — is the first of
  Anne's great bereavements

## The Critical Dramatic Question: Did Anne Witness the Crucifixion?

**No — by most traditions, Anne died before the Passion.**

- Anne is never depicted with the adult Christ in iconography or tradition
- She was regarded as having died during the youth of Jesus
- One tradition places her death at around age 62, approximately 12 AD —
  when Jesus was a young adolescent
- Some traditions say she died in Mary's arms, with Jesus and Joseph present
- The exact circumstances of her death are lost to history

This creates the central dramatic irony of your piece:
**Anne's grief is anticipatory, not witnessed.** She loved a grandson she would never
see grow to manhood, and she loved a daughter she knew was destined for extraordinary sorrow.
Her bereavement is the grief of foreknowledge — of shadows cast backward through time.

## Anne and Mary: The Bond

The love between Anne and Mary carried theological weight far beyond the personal.

- Anne's motherhood of Mary was understood as **mirroring Mary's motherhood of Jesus**
- She was revered as *avia Christi* — "grandmother of Christ" — and matriarch of the Holy Kinship
- The most celebrated image of this bond is the *Anna Selbdritt* (German: "herself making a third"):
  Anne seated like a throne, Mary on her knee, the infant Jesus in Mary's arms —
  three generations held in a single image of light
- This iconographic type flourished especially in northern Germany c. 1480–1520
- It was understood as a maternal echo of the Trinity

The *Anna Selbdritt* represents the only moment of completeness in the family's story.
From this point, everything moves toward surrender and loss.

## The Pietà Tradition and Anne's Absent Grief

Anne is not present in the Pietà — she was already dead. But your piece can work
with the *negative space* of that absence.

- The Pietà tradition (Mary holding the dead Christ) dates from the 4th century;
  it gained major prominence in 13th-century Germany as Marian devotion intensified
- Michelangelo's Pietà (c. 1498–99) is its supreme expression
- The theological logic of the Pietà is that Mary's grief participates in salvation —
  she holds in her arms what she once held in her womb
- Anne held Mary. Anne's act of holding mirrors and anticipates Mary's.

**The inverted Pietà:** Anne cradling a living Mary, knowing that Mary's destiny is grief —
is the compositional counterimage to Mary cradling a dead Christ.

## The Prophetic Burden: Simeon's Sword

Luke 2:34–35: Simeon, seeing the infant Jesus at the Temple Presentation, tells Mary:
*"A sword will pierce your own soul too."*

- Tradition sometimes places Anne at or near the Temple Presentation
  (she may be confused with Anna the Prophetess in Luke 2:36–38, a conflation
  with significant artistic history)
- Whether or not Anne heard Simeon's prophecy directly, she lived inside a
  prophetic tradition that expected the Messiah's suffering
- The act of presenting Mary — and later being near the infant Jesus —
  would have carried the full weight of messianic expectation

Simeon's prophecy is structurally available to your piece as a *sword motif*:
the moment joy becomes shadowed, the moment the grandmother understands
what she has brought into the world.

## Suggested Compositional Arc

1. **Barrenness and waiting** — the long grief before joy; echoing Hannah in 1 Samuel 1
2. **The Annunciation to Anne** — a private, quiet moment; almost forgotten by tradition
3. **Surrendering Mary to the Temple** — the first bereavement; giving the beloved daughter to God at age three
4. **The *Anna Selbdritt* moment** — the three generations held in light; the fullness before the fracture
5. **Foreknowledge and shadow** — Simeon's sword; the grandmother who understands what she has consecrated
6. **Anne's death** — in Mary's arms, Jesus and Joseph present; a peaceful death that nevertheless
   leaves Mary motherless as the Passion approaches
7. **The absent grief** — Anne dead before Gethsemane; her grief existing in the space of
   what she was spared; anticipatory sorrow as its own form of bereavement

## Patronages of St Anne (contextual colour)

Anne is patron of: grandmothers, mothers, women in labour, childless couples, miners
(Christ = gold, Mary = silver, Anne = the mine from which they were drawn),
seamstresses, horseback riders, and the poor.

Feast Day: **26 July** (with St Joachim) in the Roman Catholic Church
Feast Day: **25 July** in the Eastern Orthodox Church

Major shrines:
- Basilica of Sainte-Anne-de-Beaupré, Québec, Canada
- Sainte-Anne-d'Auray, Brittany, France
- St Anne's Church, Jerusalem (near the Pools of Bethesda, possible birthplace of Mary)
