A theatrical framing should reinforce two central ideas: grief experienced indirectly (St Anne suffering through another’s loss) and the instability of memory—something always shifting just out of reach. The orchestral and physical choreography can embody this.

Start by defining core characters, even if they are abstracted through performers rather than literal actors:

St Anne is not a singer but a presence. She could be represented by a principal violist or conductor-adjacent figure who never fully engages in sound production—gesturing, cueing, almost conducting but never resolving anything. Her grief is displaced.

The Bereaved (the true subject of loss) is embodied by the double bass soloist. The instrument’s physicality—carrying, spinning, slapping—makes it ideal. This character is grounded but volatile, alternating between control and collapse.

The Echo or Memory is the slide guitar. It should not sit traditionally in the orchestra but remain slightly apart—perhaps elevated or behind a scrim. Its tone should feel like a distortion of what has already occurred.

The Fugue is personified by a group of violinists, moving physically through the stage. They are restless, never synchronized, chasing each other’s lines but always arriving too late.

The Witnesses (cellos, winds) observe and occasionally intervene, but their attempts at cohesion fail.

Possible structure:

Opening scene: “The Absence”
The stage is dim, with St Anne positioned but inactive. The double bass begins alone, slow arco phrases that feel like searching. The slide guitar answers faintly, not as a call-and-response but as a delayed echo. Violinists enter one by one from different points, initiating a fragmented fugue. They begin to walk in curved paths, never intersecting cleanly.

Second scene: “Proxy Grief”
St Anne begins to gesture—subtle conducting motions—but the orchestra does not fully follow. Instead, the double bass responds physically, carrying the emotional weight. The bassist starts incorporating percussive slaps, as if interrupting their own lines. The violinists now move toward the cellos, trying to “resolve” their lines into the lower register, but each modulation shifts before they arrive. Lighting should shift with harmonic changes, reinforcing instability.

Third scene: “Distortion”
The slide guitar becomes dominant. Its lines stretch and bend the harmonic center. The double bassist spins the instrument slowly during sustained notes, creating a visual disorientation. Winds enter with sustained clusters, acting as a kind of emotional fog. St Anne attempts to “correct” the ensemble, but every cue results in further fragmentation.

Fourth scene: “The Pursuit”
The fugue becomes more literal. Violinists begin chasing each other across the stage in looping paths, passing motifs physically. At moments, one stops and plays intensely, only for another to take the line and move away. The cellos attempt to anchor them—perhaps standing and stepping forward—but fail to hold them. The double bass walks across the stage while playing, grounding the movement but never stopping it.

Fifth scene: “Collapse”
Everything contracts. The double bass drops into aggressive pizzicato and slap passages, almost dance-like but increasingly erratic. The slide guitar mirrors these gestures but stretched and mournful. The orchestra fractures into small clusters, each repeating fragments of earlier material. St Anne moves among them, attempting to connect players physically—touching shoulders, guiding bows—but nothing aligns.

Final scene: “Return Without Resolution”
The movement slows. The violinists return to their original positions, but now they play in near unison—slightly out of phase, suggesting memory rather than reality. The double bass returns to a central position, no longer moving, playing a simple line stripped of virtuosity. The slide guitar echoes one final time, but now clearly detached, almost indifferent.

St Anne remains standing, finally giving a clear, decisive cue—but no one responds. Silence follows not as closure, but as suspension.

Additional staging ideas:
The double bassist’s physicality should feel like both burden and resilience—carrying the instrument at times, leaning on it at others.
The violinists’ movement should never feel choreographed in a dance sense; it should resemble searching or missing connections.
Lighting can track harmonic modulation, subtly shifting color temperature to reflect emotional instability.
The slide guitar’s placement off-center reinforces its role as memory rather than participant.

Theatrically, the piece should avoid literal narrative resolution. The goal is to embody the experience of carrying someone else’s grief—never fully owning it, never escaping it.
