# Week 10 — The Test Piece: Putting It All Together

## Relevant Passages from the Literature

**Rimsky-Korsakov — *Principles of Orchestration*, Preface:**
> "The best way to learn orchestration is to write for orchestra and 
> hear the result. No treatise, however thorough, can substitute for 
> the experience of hearing one's own intentions either confirmed or 
> contradicted by living players in a real room. The student who has 
> written twenty short pieces for small forces and heard them played 
> has learned more than the student who has read every book and written 
> nothing."

This is the culminating lesson of your ten weeks. You have written 
short pieces and heard them played. You have accumulated exactly the 
kind of empirical knowledge Rimsky-Korsakov values above all else. 
The test piece is not an examination — it is a demonstration to 
yourself of how much that knowledge has grown.

**Walter Piston — *Orchestration*, Chapter 15:**
> "A short piece for small orchestra is the most demanding form of 
> orchestration study. There is nowhere to hide. Every balance decision, 
> every register choice, every dynamic marking is immediately audible 
> and immediately accountable. The student who can write a coherent, 
> balanced, and expressive three-minute piece for six players has 
> demonstrated a mastery of fundamentals that no amount of theoretical 
> study can replace."

Piston's standard — coherent, balanced, expressive — is the three-word 
brief for your test piece. Not ambitious, not complex, not impressive. 
Coherent, balanced, expressive.

**Samuel Adler — *The Study of Orchestration*, Chapter 16:**
> "When writing a short test or study piece, the student should resist 
> the temptation to demonstrate everything they know simultaneously. 
> The most effective short pieces are built around a single expressive 
> idea — a mood, a gesture, a colour — and orchestrated with discipline 
> and restraint. It is far better to do one thing beautifully than five 
> things adequately."

Adler's warning against demonstrating everything simultaneously is the 
single most important piece of advice for Week 10. You have learned 
ten concepts over ten weeks. The test piece does not need to contain 
all ten. Choose the three or four that resonated most deeply with you 
and build the piece around those.

**Cecil Forsyth — *Orchestration*, Chapter 1:**
> "The first question the orchestrator must ask is not 'which instruments 
> shall I use?' but 'what do I want to say?' The instrumentation follows 
> from the expressive intention — never the other way around. A composer 
> who chooses instruments first and finds something to say with them 
> afterward produces music that sounds like an orchestration exercise. 
> A composer who knows what they want to say and finds the instruments 
> that say it most naturally produces music that sounds inevitable."

Forsyth's distinction between inside-out and outside-in composition 
is the deepest principle in this entire curriculum. Start with 
expression. Let the instruments follow.

## Concept Focus
**A complete short piece for six players — one representative from 
each family explored over ten weeks — demonstrating coherent cross-family 
balance, clear functional voice assignment, idiomatic writing for every 
instrument, and a single unified expressive character.**

This is not a new concept but a synthesis of all previous concepts, 
applied simultaneously to a complete musical statement with a beginning, 
a middle, and an end.

---

## Instrumentation
**Flute · Oboe · Horn in F · Violin · Viola · Cello**

The reasoning behind each choice:

- **Flute**: Your primary woodwind melodic voice from Week 6, 
  now available as melody or colour
- **Oboe**: Your first woodwind encounter from Week 5 — its 
  penetrating quality and its balance demands are now familiar 
  to you
- **Horn**: Your cross-family bridge from Week 7, now integrated 
  naturally into a mixed ensemble
- **Violin**: The treble string voice introduced in Week 3, 
  capable of melody, harmony, and octave doubling
- **Viola**: Your original instrument from Week 1, now understood 
  across all its registers and functions
- **Cello**: The melodic bass voice that has anchored nearly every 
  week of this curriculum

No brass beyond the horn, no double bass, no percussion. This is 
deliberate — the ensemble is small enough to be manageable and 
intimate enough that every balance decision is immediately audible.

## The Test Piece Brief

### Title
Give your piece a title before you write a note. Forsyth insists 
that knowing what you want to say comes first. The title does not 
need to be poetic — it simply commits you to an expressive 
intention. Suggestions if you need a starting point:
- *Notturno* — a quiet, nocturnal character
- *Canzona* — a song-like, flowing character  
- *Intrada* — a ceremonial, processional character
- *Elegia* — a lamenting, reflective character

Or choose your own. But choose before bar one.

### Duration
**1 minute 30 seconds to 2 minutes** at your chosen tempo. 
This is approximately 24–36 bars at ♩ = 60–80. Do not exceed 
this. Piston's standard of coherence is harder to maintain 
the longer the piece runs.

### Structure
A simple **A – B – A'** form:

**A Section (bars 1–10): Establishment**
- Introduce your primary melodic voice and your harmonic 
  background simultaneously
- Establish tempo, character, and dynamic level clearly 
  in the first two bars
- Use no more than three instruments in the opening bars — 
  add the remaining voices gradually
- Dynamic range: *p* to *mp*

**B Section (bars 11–20): Contrast**
- Introduce one element of contrast — a different register, 
  a different dynamic level, a different functional 
  assignment for one instrument, or a brief modulation 
  to a closely related key
- This is where Adler's single moment of independent 
  violin movement from Week 9 finds its natural home — 
  one instrument stepping out of its background role 
  briefly before returning
- Dynamic range: *mp* to *mf* — this is the piece's 
  expressive peak

**A' Section (bars 21–end): Return and Resolution**
- Return to the opening material, slightly varied — 
  a different instrument carries the melody, or the 
  harmony is redistributed across the voices
- Diminuendo to *p* and then *pp* in the final four bars
- Final chord: all six instruments, open position, 
  sustained, *pp* — let it decay naturally

### Functional Assignments
Before writing, complete this table for each section:

| Instrument | A Section    | B Section      | A' Section   |
|------------|--------------|----------------|--------------|
| Flute      | MELODY       | COLOUR         | HARMONY      |
| Oboe       | HARMONY      | MELODY         | COLOUR       |
| Horn       | COLOUR       | HARMONY        | COLOUR       |
| Violin     | HARMONY      | COUNTER-MELODY | MELODY       |
| Viola      | INNER HARMONY| INNER HARMONY  | INNER HARMONY|
| Cello      | BASS         | BASS           | BASS         |

This is a suggested assignment — adjust it to suit your expressive 
intention. But every instrument must have a clearly assigned function 
in every section, and no two instruments should have the same function 
simultaneously unless doubling is deliberate and purposeful.

### Orchestration Checklist
Before presenting the piece to the players, verify each item:

**Register**
- [ ] Every instrument stays within its practical, comfortable range
- [ ] No instrument is written consistently in its weakest register
- [ ] Registral separation between families is maintained 
      except at deliberate crossing points
- [ ] Horn transposition checked on every note

**Balance**
- [ ] Oboe dynamic is lower than the string dynamic in every 
      passage where it accompanies
- [ ] Horn remains at *pp* to *mp* except at the expressive peak
- [ ] No two instruments double at the unison unless the 
      doubling serves a specific colour or strength purpose
- [ ] The cello bass line is audible but never dominant

**Voice-leading**
- [ ] No voice crosses below the cello or above the flute
- [ ] Inner voices (viola, horn) move smoothly without 
      large leaps
- [ ] The oboe has at least one natural breath point 
      every four bars

**Notation**
- [ ] Every part has a tempo marking and a dynamic at bar 1
- [ ] All dynamic changes are marked in every part simultaneously 
      — not just in the melody
- [ ] Articulation markings are consistent across parts
- [ ] The score is transposed correctly — horn in F, and 
      Bb oboe if applicable (concert oboe does not transpose — 
      verify with your player)
- [ ] Parts are extracted and legible — do not ask players 
      to read from a full score in a 10-minute session

## Presenting the Piece to the Players

### Before the Reading
- Distribute parts two to three minutes before you want 
  to start — give players time to scan the music quietly
- Give a single sentence of expressive context: *"This is 
  a quiet, nocturnal piece — the mood should feel settled 
  and unhurried throughout"*. No more than one sentence. 
  Trust the music and the players.
- Ask for one read-through from beginning to end without 
  stopping, even if there are errors. Your first priority 
  is to hear the whole shape.

### During the Reading
- Do not conduct unless absolutely necessary for tempo
- Listen actively: take mental notes on balance, register, 
  and any moments where the texture clouds or clarifies
- Watch the players' body language — difficulty in a part 
  is visible before it is audible

### After the Reading
Ask these specific questions, in this order:

1. *"Was there any moment where you felt your part was 
   uncomfortable to play — awkward fingering, register 
   strain, or an unnatural phrase shape?"*
2. *"From inside the ensemble, could you hear the primary 
   melody clearly in every section?"*
3. *"Was there a dynamic marking that felt wrong — either 
   too loud or too quiet relative to what the music seemed 
   to want?"*
4. *"If you could change one bar anywhere in the piece — 
   just one — which would it be and why?"*
5. *"Did the piece feel like it had a shape — a beginning, 
   a middle, and an end — or did it feel like an exercise?"*

That last question is the most important. Piston's standard 
was coherent, balanced, expressive. The players will tell 
you honestly which of those three you achieved.

---

## What to Observe & Validate

1. **Coherence** — does the piece feel like a single unified 
   statement, or a series of unrelated exercises stitched 
   together? This is the structural question.
2. **Balance across all six instruments** — is there any 
   moment where a single instrument dominates unexpectedly, 
   or disappears entirely? Apply everything you learned in 
   Weeks 5, 6, and 7 about dynamic asymmetry across families.
3. **Functional clarity** — in each section, is the primary 
   melodic voice immediately identifiable? Do the supporting 
   voices genuinely support?
4. **Idiomatic writing** — do the players look comfortable? 
   Nine weeks of idiomatic study should show in the naturalness 
   of each part.
5. **Expressive character** — does the piece have the character 
   you intended when you chose its title? If not, where did 
   the intention and the notation diverge?

## Reflection: What You Have Learned

Over ten weeks you have tested, in a live acoustic environment 
with real players, the following:

- The range, register character, and idiomatic writing 
  demands of seven orchestral instruments
- The blending properties of the lower string choir
- Two-voice independence and pedal point writing
- Octave doubling and its effect on melodic weight
- The four-part string texture and divisi writing
- Dynamic balance and acoustic asymmetry between woodwinds 
  and strings
- Woodwind colour mixing and the chemistry of timbre
- The horn as a cross-family blending and colouring voice
- The brass choir's dynamic range and registral demands
- Full cross-family synthesis with functional voice assignment

Rimsky-Korsakov said the student who has written twenty short 
pieces and heard them played has learned more than the student 
who has read every book and written nothing. You have written 
nine short pieces and one complete test piece, and heard all 
of them played. That is the foundation of an orchestrator.

The books on your shelf — Rimsky-Korsakov, Piston, Adler, 
Forsyth — now mean something different than they did in Week 1. 
You have context for their advice. You have heard, in a real 
room with real players, what they were describing. Return to 
them now and read them again. They will read differently.

## Next Steps Beyond Week 10

This curriculum has covered the fundamentals. The natural 
next steps, when you are ready, are:

- **Expand the ensemble** — add a second violin, a double 
  bass, a clarinet, or a trumpet and begin testing larger 
  combinations
- **Study scores** — take the pieces in your orchestration 
  library and analyse them bar by bar against what you 
  now know from live experience
- **Write longer** — extend your forms from ABA to 
  something with more development, more modulation, 
  more textural variety
- **Return to the maestro** — share the test piece score 
  with the conductor and ask for a full analytical 
  conversation about what works and what does not
- **Read Berlioz** — the *Treatise on Instrumentation* 
  will now make sense in a way it could not have in 
  Week 1. It is the deepest and most passionate of all 
  the orchestration texts, and it rewards a reader who 
  has already heard their own music played.
