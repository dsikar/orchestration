# Week 9 — Synthesis: All Three Families Together

## Relevant Passages from the Literature

**Rimsky-Korsakov — *Principles of Orchestration*, Chapter 4:**
> "The moment all three families sound simultaneously, the entire question 
> of balance is transformed. What worked in isolation may not work in 
> combination. The orchestrator must now think vertically across the entire 
> orchestral spectrum rather than horizontally within a single family. This 
> is the true beginning of orchestration — everything before it is 
> preparation."

This is perhaps the most important single observation in the entire 
treatise for your purposes this week. The previous eight weeks have 
been preparation. This week is where orchestration actually begins.

**Walter Piston — *Orchestration*, Chapter 12:**
> "The great danger of the full orchestral tutti is not noise but mud. 
> When every family plays simultaneously at similar dynamic levels, 
> the individual voices lose definition and the result is an 
> undifferentiated mass of sound. Clarity in the tutti is achieved 
> not by reducing the number of instruments but by ensuring that each 
> family occupies a distinct registral layer and fulfils a distinct 
> functional role — melody, harmony, bass, or colour."

Piston's four functional roles — melody, harmony, bass, and colour — 
are the structural framework for this week's exercise. Every instrument 
you write for must be assigned one of these four functions clearly and 
consciously.

**Samuel Adler — *The Study of Orchestration*, Chapter 14:**
> "The most reliable principle for combining all three orchestral 
> families is the principle of registral separation. Give each family 
> its own layer of the sonic spectrum — strings in the middle, 
> woodwinds above, brass below or at the foundation — and each 
> will speak clearly. Violate this principle and the families begin 
> to compete rather than collaborate."

Adler immediately qualifies this by noting that the most interesting 
orchestral writing deliberately violates registral separation at 
carefully chosen moments — but insists that you must understand the 
rule before you can break it productively. This week you follow 
the rule.

**Cecil Forsyth — *Orchestration*, Chapter 11:**
> "The string body, when playing at mezzo-forte in its middle register, 
> forms the ideal neutral background against which woodwind and brass 
> colours can be heard with maximum clarity. Think of the strings as 
> the canvas and the winds as the paint. A busy, active string part 
> is a textured canvas — interesting in itself, but less receptive 
> to the colours laid over it. A sustained, homophonic string part 
> is a smooth canvas — less interesting alone, but it lets every 
> wind colour shine."

Forsyth's canvas metaphor is directly applicable to the texture you 
will write this week — sustained strings as background, woodwind and 
brass colours foregrounded against them.

---

## Concept Focus
**Full orchestral synthesis — combining strings, woodwinds, and brass 
in a single coherent passage, with each instrument assigned a clear 
functional role (melody, harmony, bass, or colour), each family 
occupying a distinct registral layer, and balance managed consciously 
across all three families simultaneously.**

Everything you have learned over the previous eight weeks converges 
here. Register awareness from Weeks 1–3, four-part texture from Week 4, 
dynamic balance from Week 5, woodwind colour from Week 6, horn blending 
from Week 7, and brass character from Week 8 — all of it is needed 
simultaneously for the first time.

---

## Recommended Player Group
**Flute · Horn · Violin · Cello**

Four players, four families represented, four distinct functional 
roles available. This is the smallest ensemble in which true 
cross-family synthesis can be meaningfully tested. The flute 
carries the woodwind voice, the horn bridges brass and strings 
as it has done since Week 7, the violin provides the string 
treble, and the cello provides the string bass. Ask players 
you have already worked with where possible — familiarity with 
your working method will make the 10-minute session more 
productive.

## Deliverable: MUSIC

### The Exercise
Write a single, carefully considered passage of **16 bars** — 
the longest single excerpt you have written in this series — 
for flute, horn in F, violin, and cello.

### Functional Assignment (follow this strictly)
Before writing a single note, assign each instrument its 
function for the entire passage:

- **Flute**: MELODY — the primary melodic voice throughout. 
  Lyrical, singable, in the flute's warm middle register 
  (A4–E6). This is Forsyth's paint on the canvas.
- **Violin**: HARMONY — sustained inner harmony notes, 
  supporting the flute melody from below. Mostly half notes 
  and whole notes. Stay within D4–A5. This is Forsyth's canvas.
- **Horn**: COLOUR — long sustained notes that add warmth 
  and blend without asserting. The same colourist role 
  from Week 7. Written C4–G4, sounding F3–C4. *pp* to 
  *mp* only.
- **Cello**: BASS — root-position harmonic foundation, 
  one note per bar, half notes and whole notes. The same 
  anchoring role from Week 1. Stay within D2–A3.

### Structure
Divide the 16 bars into three sections:

**Bars 1–4 — Establishment**
- All four instruments enter together, *mp*
- Flute states the opening phrase of the melody
- Violin, horn, and cello establish their supporting roles
- Keep dynamics stable — no crescendo or diminuendo yet

**Bars 5–12 — Development**
- Flute melody continues and develops — introduce one small 
  leap, one moment of rhythmic interest
- At bar 7: violin adds a single moment of independent 
  movement — one bar where it moves in quarter notes rather 
  than sustained harmony, then returns to long notes
- At bar 9: horn makes a small crescendo from *pp* to *mp* 
  — test what this adds to the colour, then return to *pp*
- Cello remains steady throughout — the anchor

**Bars 13–16 — Resolution**
- All voices diminuendo to *p* by bar 14
- Flute melody resolves stepwise to the tonic
- Horn, violin, and cello hold a final sustained chord — 
  open position, root-fifth-third across the three voices
- Final bar: all instruments *pp*, held until natural decay

### Notation Tips
- Write each part on its own staff and label functions 
  at the top: *"Flute — melody"*, *"Violin — harmony"* etc. 
  This is for your own clarity as much as the players'
- Horn transposition: remember every note sounds a perfect 
  fifth lower than written
- Keep the flute melody within a singable, vocal character — 
  if you cannot sing it yourself, simplify it
- Mark all dynamics clearly in every part at every change — 
  do not assume players will infer dynamics from context
- Write in G major or E minor — natural, resonant keys 
  for all four instruments in these registers

## What to Observe & Validate

1. **Registral separation** — can you hear all four voices 
   simultaneously without any one masking another? Is Adler's 
   principle of registral layering borne out in practice?
2. **Functional clarity** — is the flute melody immediately 
   identifiable as the primary voice? Do the supporting 
   instruments genuinely support rather than compete?
3. **Canvas effect** — does the sustained violin harmony 
   function as Forsyth's smooth canvas, making the flute 
   colour more vivid? Or does the violin part feel too 
   passive and underwritten?
4. **Horn colour addition** — at bar 9 when the horn 
   crescendos to *mp*, what exactly changes in the texture? 
   Can you describe the colour shift in words?
5. **Cross-family blend** — does this four-instrument 
   combination sound like a unified ensemble, or like 
   four separate instruments playing independently? 
   What creates or prevents the sense of ensemble?
6. **Balance across families** — is any single instrument 
   consistently too loud or too quiet throughout? Note 
   exactly which bars and which instruments — this data 
   feeds directly into the Week 10 test piece.

Ask the players:
- "As an ensemble, did the four parts feel balanced from 
  the inside, or was there a voice you struggled to hear?"
- "Flautist — did the sustained string and horn support 
  beneath you change how you played the melody, compared 
  to playing alone?"
- "Horn — with strings present alongside you, did your 
  blending role feel natural, or did you feel pulled toward 
  projecting more?"
- "Cellist — did the bass function feel satisfying, or 
  did you feel under-utilised compared to what you played 
  in the earlier weeks?"
- "If you were to change one thing about the balance or 
  texture, what would it be?"

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Overloading the texture** — the instinct when writing 
   for multiple families simultaneously is to fill every 
   register and every beat. Resist this. Silence and 
   sustained notes in the supporting voices are what 
   allow the melody to breathe. Piston's warning about 
   mud applies even to four instruments.
2. **Neglecting the cello bass** — after weeks of writing 
   interesting cello lines, the bass function can feel like 
   a demotion. It is not. A well-placed, well-timed bass 
   note is the harmonic anchor that makes everything above 
   it meaningful. Treat it with the same care as the melody.
3. **Forgetting what you learned in earlier weeks** — each 
   of the four instruments in this exercise was explored 
   in isolation in a previous week. Return to those weeks' 
   notes before writing. The register warnings, the 
   dynamic asymmetries, the transposition checks — all 
   of it applies simultaneously now.

## Follow-Up Note → Week 10
Week 9 has given you your first complete cross-family texture 
and — crucially — a live reading of it. Take detailed notes 
during and immediately after the session: what balanced well, 
what did not, what surprised you, what the players said. 
These notes are the primary material for the Week 10 test 
piece. As Rimsky-Korsakov says, this week is where 
orchestration truly begins — and Week 10 is where you 
demonstrate, in a short but complete piece, what you have 
learned from that beginning.

## Maestro Discussion Option
If the music is not ready, bring these questions to the maestro:

1. "When you look at a full orchestral score for the first 
   time, what is the first thing your eye goes to in terms 
   of balance and texture?"
2. "Piston talks about the danger of mud in a tutti — how 
   do you identify and fix textural congestion in rehearsal?"
3. "Is Adler's principle of registral separation something 
   you actively coach composers toward, or do you find the 
   best orchestrators arrive at it instinctively?"
4. "When all three families play together, which balance 
   problem do you encounter most often in student or 
   amateur scores?"
5. "Looking ahead to Week 10 — if I were to write a short 
   test piece for four to six players drawing on strings, 
   woodwinds, and brass, what single piece of advice would 
   you give me before I start?"
