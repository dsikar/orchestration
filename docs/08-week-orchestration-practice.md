# Week 8 — The Brass Choir: Trumpet & Trombone

## Relevant Passages from the Literature

**Cecil Forsyth — *Orchestration*, Chapter 8:**
> "Where the horn is a bridge, the trumpet is a declaration. It does 
> not blend so much as it cuts. Even at piano the trumpet retains an 
> edge, a presence, a quality of announcement that no other instrument 
> in the orchestra possesses. The orchestrator who forgets this and 
> writes the trumpet as if it were a loud oboe will always be surprised 
> by the result."

Forsyth follows this with a practical observation that the trumpet's 
penetrating quality is not merely a matter of volume but of harmonic 
content — the trumpet's tone is rich in upper partials at every dynamic 
level, which is why it cuts through textures that would mask any other 
instrument.

**Rimsky-Korsakov — *Principles of Orchestration*, Chapter 3:**
> "The brass instruments as a group form the most powerful choir in the 
> orchestra. But power is not their only quality — used softly, in 
> sustained chords, they produce a richness and solemnity that no other 
> combination of instruments can match. The student should learn to think 
> of the brass not only as a force for climax and declaration, but as a 
> vehicle for the deepest and most solemn kinds of beauty."

He goes on to describe the ideal four-part brass chord — two horns, 
trumpet, and trombone — as the single most satisfying vertical sonority 
in the orchestra when voiced correctly in open position. This week you 
work toward exactly that sonority, beginning with just two of its voices.

**Walter Piston — *Orchestration*, Chapter 10:**
> "The trombone is the most direct and honest instrument in the orchestra. 
> It says exactly what it means at every dynamic level. At forte it is 
> magnificent and overpowering; at piano it is dark, solemn, and noble. 
> It has no register of weakness, no colour that is unclear or ambiguous. 
> This directness makes it both powerful and inflexible — it cannot 
> dissemble, and the orchestrator cannot hide poor voice-leading behind 
> its tone."

Piston's point about the trombone's honesty is a practical warning as 
much as a description: poor voice-leading in a trombone part is 
immediately and brutally audible in a way that it might not be on a 
string instrument.

**Samuel Adler — *The Study of Orchestration*, Chapter 11:**
> "The most common mistake in student brass writing is the chord voiced 
> in close position throughout. Brass instruments in close position — 
> all voices within the span of an octave — produce a thick, congested 
> tone that quickly becomes fatiguing to both players and listeners. 
> Open position — voices spread across two octaves — allows each 
> instrument to speak clearly in its most resonant register and produces 
> the characteristic brass richness that composers from Beethoven to 
> Bruckner exploited so magnificently."

Adler's open-versus-close-position guidance for brass directly parallels 
Forsyth's string spacing advice from Week 4 — a reminder that register 
and spacing principles are universal across instrument families.

---

## Concept Focus
**The brass choir in its simplest form — trumpet and trombone as two 
contrasting voices — exploring open position voicing, dynamic range 
from noble piano to powerful forte, and the timbral contrast between 
the trumpet's cutting brilliance and the trombone's dark solemnity.**

Having spent Week 7 with the horn as a hidden blending voice, you now 
encounter the brass in their more declarative roles. The trumpet and 
trombone are chosen as this week's pair because they represent the 
two extremes of brass character — Forsyth's announcement versus 
Piston's solemnity — and because their contrast is immediately and 
dramatically audible even in a short 10-minute reading session.

---

## Recommended Player Group
**Trumpet · Trombone · Horn**

Retaining the horn from last week alongside the new voices serves two 
purposes: it gives you a direct comparison between the horn's blending 
quality and the trumpet's cutting presence within the same session, 
and it begins to suggest the full brass choir that Rimsky-Korsakov 
describes. Ask the principal trumpet, principal trombone, and the same 
horn player from Week 7 if possible — continuity with the horn player 
means they can comment on how the new brass voices change the acoustic 
environment.

## Deliverable: MUSIC

### The Exercise
Write **three short passages of 6–8 bars each**, each exploring a 
different facet of the trumpet-trombone relationship.

### Passage A — Unison Melody, Dynamic Contrast
- **Trumpet**: A bold, simple melody of 8 bars in its middle register 
  (E4–C6). Mostly stepwise with one or two assertive leaps of a fourth 
  or fifth. Strong rhythmic profile — dotted rhythms or clear quarter 
  note patterns work well.
- **Trombone**: Doubles the trumpet melody exactly one octave below 
  (same rhythm, same notes, octave displacement). Stay within 
  E2–C4 — Adler's resonant middle register for trombone.
- **Horn**: Sustained root-position harmony notes below both, 
  whole notes, *pp* — the same blending role as last week.
- **Version 1**: All brass *mf*
- **Version 2**: All brass *p*
- **Listen for**: How dramatically does the character change between 
  *mf* and *p*? At *p* does the trombone become Piston's "dark and 
  noble" voice? At *mf* does the trumpet begin to assert Forsyth's 
  "quality of announcement"?

### Passage B — Independent Voices, Open Position
- **Trumpet**: A sustained melodic line in its middle register 
  (G4–D5). Long notes — half notes and whole notes. *mp*.
- **Trombone**: An independent counter-line well below the trumpet — 
  at least a tenth lower — in its warmest register (Bb2–F3). 
  Moving against the trumpet: when trumpet holds, trombone moves 
  and vice versa. *mp*.
- **Horn**: Fills a middle harmony note between the two voices, 
  sustained whole notes, *p*.
- This is Adler's open position — voices spread across two octaves, 
  each instrument in its most resonant register.
- **Listen for**: Does the open spacing produce the clarity and 
  richness Adler describes? Can you hear three independent voices, 
  or do they merge into a single brass sonority?

### Passage C — Dynamic Swell, Full Brass Colour
- **All three instruments**: Begin *pp*, crescendo over 8 bars to 
  *ff*, then diminuendo back to *pp* in the final 2 bars.
- **Trumpet**: Sustained held note — the fifth of the harmony — 
  throughout. No melody, no rhythm. Just a sustained pitch.
- **Trombone**: Sustained held note — the third of the harmony — 
  a sixth below the trumpet.
- **Horn**: Sustained root note — the foundation of the chord — 
  a fifth below the trombone.
- This is a pure dynamic swell on a single brass chord — the 
  simplest possible way to hear the brass family's full dynamic 
  range from whisper to roar in a single gesture.
- Write the chord in F major: trumpet on C5, trombone on A3, 
  horn on F3 — open position, resonant, natural fingerings 
  for all three instruments.

### Notation Tips
- The trumpet in C does not transpose — what you write is what 
  you hear. If using Bb trumpet, it sounds a major second lower 
  than written — check which instrument the player uses and 
  notate accordingly. Ask before the session.
- The trombone does not transpose — written pitch equals sounding 
  pitch. Bass clef throughout.
- Keep trumpet writing below high C (C6) this week — above that 
  register the trumpet becomes extremely bright and fatiguing 
  and will overpower everything else in a small room.
- Mark all three passages clearly with letters A, B, and C 
  and a one-line description of the exercise at the top of 
  each: *"Passage A — Unison melody, two dynamics"* etc.
- Write in F major or Bb major — natural, resonant keys for 
  all brass instruments

## What to Observe & Validate
1. **Trumpet's cutting quality** — even at *p* in Passage A, 
   does the trumpet retain Forsyth's "quality of announcement"? 
   Does it ever fully recede into the background, or is it 
   always present?
2. **Trombone's dark nobility** — at *p* in Passage A Version 2, 
   does the trombone become Piston's "dark and solemn" voice? 
   How different does it feel from the same notes at *mf*?
3. **Open position richness** — in Passage B, does the wide 
   spacing produce the clarity Adler describes, compared to 
   the closer voicing of Passage A?
4. **The dynamic swell** — in Passage C, at what dynamic level 
   does the brass chord begin to feel overwhelming in the 
   rehearsal room? Remember the room is not a concert hall — 
   the *ff* may feel much louder than it would on stage.
5. **Horn as mediator** — with all three instruments present, 
   does the horn continue to function as a blending voice, 
   or does it begin to assert its own colour alongside the 
   trumpet and trombone?

Ask the players:
- "Trumpet — at what dynamic do you feel you shift from 
  blending to leading? Is it the same in every register?"
- "Trombone — does Passage B's open position feel more 
  natural to play than the closer voicing of Passage A?"
- "Horn — now that the trumpet and trombone are present, 
  do you feel your role change compared to last week when 
  you were the only brass voice?"
- "All three — in Passage C, at what point in the crescendo 
  did you feel the chord tip from beautiful to overpowering?"

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Writing the trumpet too high** — above C6 the trumpet 
   becomes intensely bright and physically demanding. In a 
   small rehearsal room at close range this can be genuinely 
   uncomfortable for everyone present. Stay within the 
   middle register this week.
2. **Close position brass voicing** — as Adler warns, all 
   brass voices compressed within an octave produces a thick, 
   congested sound. In Passage B especially, make sure there 
   is at least a tenth between trumpet and trombone.
3. **Ignoring player fatigue** — brass players tire more 
   quickly than string players in a sight-reading context, 
   particularly in the upper registers. Keep passages short 
   and build in natural rest points. A horn player who has 
   been sustaining *pp* chords for three passages will 
   struggle to control dynamics accurately by the third 
   reading.

## Follow-Up Note → Week 9
You now have direct experience of strings across four weeks, 
woodwinds across two weeks, and brass across two weeks. Week 9 
is the first week of synthesis — bringing all three families 
together in a single short passage. As Rimsky-Korsakov writes, 
the moment all three families sound simultaneously, the entire 
question of balance is transformed: what worked in isolation 
may not work in combination, and the orchestrator must now 
think vertically across the entire orchestral spectrum rather 
than horizontally within a single family. This is where 
everything you have learned becomes simultaneously relevant.

## Maestro Discussion Option
If the music is not ready, bring these questions to the maestro:

1. "Forsyth describes the trumpet as a declaration rather than 
   a blend — how do you manage a principal trumpet player's 
   natural instinct to project in passages where the trumpet 
   should be subordinate?"
2. "Piston says the trombone cannot dissemble — that poor 
   voice-leading is immediately audible. Do you find that 
   to be true in rehearsal, and how does it affect how you 
   approach correcting brass writing?"
3. "Is there a rule of thumb for brass voicing in open versus 
   close position, or is it always context-dependent?"
4. "How do you think about brass fatigue in programming — 
   does it affect how you pace a rehearsal or a concert?"
5. "Can you point me to a passage in our current repertoire 
   where the trumpet and trombone have genuinely independent 
   and contrasting roles — not just doubling the same line 
   at different octaves?"
