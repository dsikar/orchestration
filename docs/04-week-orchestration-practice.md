# Week 4 — The Four-Part String Texture & Divisi Writing

## Relevant Passages from the Literature

**Walter Piston — *Orchestration*, Chapter 3:**
> "The four-part string texture — first violin, second violin, viola, cello — 
> is the bedrock of orchestral writing. The composer who has not thoroughly 
> explored what these four voices can do in every combination has not yet begun 
> to understand the orchestra."

Piston dedicates an entire chapter to what he calls "the self-sufficient string 
orchestra" — arguing that a composer who can write idiomatically for strings in 
four parts has mastered the single most transferable skill in orchestration. 
Everything learned here applies directly when winds and brass are added later.

**Rimsky-Korsakov — *Principles of Orchestration*, Chapter 1:**
> "Divisi writing — the division of a single string section into two or more 
> independent voices — is one of the most beautiful resources available to the 
> orchestrator. It transforms a unison section into a choir, and multiplies the 
> expressive possibilities without adding a single extra player."

He goes on to caution that divisi writing thins the tone of each individual 
line, since fewer bows are playing each note. This is something you can test 
directly this week by asking your players to play first as a unison, then divided.

**Samuel Adler — *The Study of Orchestration*, Chapter 4:**
> "The second violin is perhaps the most underappreciated instrument in the 
> orchestra. Student composers frequently treat it as a mere doubling of the 
> first violin, robbing it of its own character. In reality the second violin 
> occupies a unique middle ground — brighter than the viola, warmer and less 
> exposed than the first violin — and deserves melodic and harmonic writing 
> of its own."

This is the central challenge of Week 4: giving the second violin a genuine 
identity within the four-part texture rather than treating it as a shadow of 
the first.

**Cecil Forsyth — *Orchestration*, Chapter 2:**
> "Close position — all four string voices within the span of an octave — 
> produces a thick, warm, almost organ-like blend. Open position — voices 
> spread across two octaves or more — produces clarity, transparency, and 
> the sense of space that is uniquely orchestral. The student should practise 
> both, and learn to hear the difference immediately."

Forsyth's distinction between close and open position is the architectural 
principle underlying this week's exercise.

---

## Concept Focus
**Completing the four-part string texture by adding the second violin, and 
exploring divisi writing as a means of expanding harmonic richness without 
increasing the number of players.**

This is the week Piston's "bedrock" becomes fully available to you. With all 
four string voices present — first violin, second violin, viola, cello — you 
can now write complete four-part harmony, experiment with voice spacing, and 
begin to understand how divisi transforms a section from a unison instrument 
into an internal choir.

---

## Recommended Player Group
**First Violin · Second Violin · Viola**

Leave the cello and bass aside this week. By removing the bass foundation you 
are already familiar with, you force yourself to hear the upper three voices 
with fresh ears, and you isolate the question of how the second violin fits 
between first violin and viola. You can approach the section principals, or 
two desk-partners from the violin section plus the principal viola.

---

## Deliverable: MUSIC

### The Exercise
Write **two short passages of 8 bars each** — same harmonic content, 
two different approaches to spacing and division.

### Passage A — Four-Part Close Position
- **First Violin**: A simple, four-bar melodic phrase repeated twice. 
  Stay within E4–E6. Mostly stepwise with one or two small leaps.
- **Second Violin**: An inner voice a third or fourth below the first violin, 
  moving in similar rhythm. Keep it within B3–B5.
- **Viola**: A sustained inner harmony below the second violin — mostly 
  half notes and whole notes, filling in the chord. Stay within E3–E5.
- **All three instruments**: Stay within the span of roughly an octave and 
  a half — this is Forsyth's close position
- **Dynamic**: *mp*, legato throughout

### Passage B — Divisi Viola, Open Position
- **First Violin**: Exactly the same melody as Passage A, one octave higher 
  than before (up an octave from Passage A)
- **Second Violin**: Same inner voice as Passage A, unchanged
- **Viola divisi**: Split the viola into two voices:
  - **Viola I**: Takes the line the viola played in Passage A
  - **Viola II**: Adds a new voice a third below Viola I — a fifth voice 
    that only exists because of the divisi
- This spreads the texture across a wider range — Forsyth's open position

### Notation Tips
- Mark divisi clearly in the viola part: write **"div."** at the split point 
  and **"unis."** when they return to unison
- In Passage B, write Viola I and Viola II on the same staff, stems up and 
  stems down respectively
- Keep all writing within comfortable sight-reading range — no extended 
  techniques this week
- Write in D major or B minor for natural string resonance

---

## What to Observe & Validate
1. **Close vs open position** — does Passage B feel more spacious and 
   transparent than Passage A, as Forsyth predicts?
2. **Second violin identity** — does the second violin line feel like its 
   own voice, or does it cling too closely to the first violin? This is 
   Adler's test.
3. **Divisi thinning** — as Rimsky-Korsakov warns, the divided viola will 
   sound thinner than a unison viola. Is that thinning a problem, or does 
   it suit the open texture of Passage B?
4. **Voice-leading** — do all three (or four, with divisi) voices move 
   smoothly without awkward leaps or crossing?
5. **Blend across positions** — in Passage B with the wider spacing, do 
   the voices still blend, or do they begin to sound like separate 
   instruments rather than a choir?

Ask the players:
- "Does the divisi feel natural — is the split comfortable for the section 
  to execute in a sight-reading situation?"
- "Does the second violin line feel like it has its own character, or does 
  it feel like it is serving the first violin?"
- "In Passage B, does the wider spacing change how you listen to each other 
  within the ensemble?"

---

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Voice crossing** — when four voices are compressed into close position, 
   it is easy to accidentally write the second violin below the viola, or 
   the viola above the second violin. Check every bar for crossed voices.
2. **Treating second violin as a unison double** — as Adler emphasises, the 
   second violin deserves its own melodic identity. If your second violin 
   simply doubles the first violin an octave below for the entire passage, 
   you have learned nothing new from this week.
3. **Divisi too low on the viola** — Viola II in the divisi passage must not 
   drop below open C3. In a divided section with fewer bows, the lowest 
   register becomes even muddier than usual.

## Follow-Up Note → Week 5
You now have the full four-part string texture at your disposal, and you 
understand both close and open position spacing. Week 5 introduces **dynamics 
and balance across instrument families** — specifically, what happens when you 
add a single woodwind voice to your string writing. As Piston notes, the moment 
a wind instrument enters, the entire question of balance changes: a single oboe 
at *mf* can cut through a full string section at *mp* with ease, and the 
orchestrator must learn to manage this asymmetry.

## Maestro Discussion Option
If the music is not ready, bring these questions to the maestro:

1. "How do you coach the second violin section to maintain its own identity 
   when the first violin is carrying the melody above them?"
2. "When you see divisi writing in a score, what is the first thing you check 
   to make sure it will work in the room?"
3. "Is there a rule of thumb for when to use close position versus open 
   position in string writing?"
4. "How does the size of the string section affect divisi — does it work 
   differently in a chamber orchestra versus a full symphony orchestra?"
5. "Can you point me to a passage in our current repertoire where the 
   second violin section has a genuinely independent and interesting role?"
