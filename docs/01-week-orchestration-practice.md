# Week 1 — The Low Strings: Getting to Know the Bass Foundation

## Relevant Passages from the Literature

**Rimsky-Korsakov — *Principles of Orchestration*, Chapter 1:**
> "The strings form the most important group in the orchestra. They are the most 
> flexible, the most expressive, and the most homogeneous in quality of tone."

He opens his entire treatise with the string family precisely because he considers 
them the irreducible foundation of orchestral thought. For a composer beginning 
their orchestration journey, he implicitly argues, there is no better starting 
point than the strings — and no better starting point within the strings than 
the bass voices.

**Cecil Forsyth — *Orchestration*, Chapter 2 (The Strings):**
> "The double bass is the foundation-stone of the orchestra. Remove it, and the 
> whole edifice becomes top-heavy and insecure. Its function is not merely to 
> reinforce the cello at the lower octave — it is to provide the acoustic 
> ground on which everything else rests."

Forsyth goes on to note that the double bass is the most frequently mishandled 
instrument by student composers, who either write it too high (losing its 
foundational character) or double it so closely with the cello that it adds 
nothing distinctive.

**Samuel Adler — *The Study of Orchestration*, Chapter 2:**
> "Register is not merely a matter of pitch — it is a matter of character. 
> Each instrument has a personality in each of its registers, and the composer 
> who ignores this writes for instruments as if they were organ pipes — identical 
> in colour at every pitch."

Adler uses the cello as his primary example here, distinguishing between its 
warm, singing upper register (thumb position, A string) and its darker, weightier 
lower register (open C and G strings) — both of which you will hear this week.

**Walter Piston — *Orchestration*, Chapter 3:**
> "The practice of writing for the lower strings as a self-contained choir — 
> viola, cello, and double bass — is one of the oldest and most reliable textures 
> in the orchestral repertoire. The student composer is well advised to begin here, 
> where the principles of balance, blend, and register are most clearly audible 
> and most immediately verifiable."

This is almost a direct endorsement of exactly what you are doing in Week 1.

---

## Concept Focus
Individual instrument ranges, registers, and idiomatic writing for the **double 
bass, cello, and viola** — the three instruments you will approach this week. 
Understanding how each instrument sits in its register, what sounds natural under 
the fingers, and what each voice contributes to a low-string texture.

---

## Recommended Player Group
**Double Bass · Cello · Viola**

This is the ideal starting trio because:
- Together they form the complete lower string choir
- The contrast between viola (tenor/alto), cello (bass/tenor), and double bass 
  (sub-bass) is immediately audible
- Low strings are forgiving for a first reading — players are accustomed to 
  sight-reading simple sustained passages
- You can hear balance, blend, and register contrast all at once in a quiet setting

---

## Deliverable: MUSIC

### The Exercise
Write a single passage of **8–12 bars** for viola, cello, and double bass.

### What to Write
- **Tempo**: Slow — ♩ = 52–60. This gives you time to hear everything clearly.
- **Time signature**: 4/4
- **Texture**: Homophonic — all three instruments moving together, mostly in 
  long notes (half notes and whole notes)
- **Content**:
  - Give the **cello** a simple, stepwise melodic line in its middle register 
    (roughly D3–A4)
  - Give the **viola** a sustained harmony note above the cello — a third or a 
    fifth above
  - Give the **double bass** the root of each implied harmony, one note per bar, 
    arco (bowed)
- **Dynamic**: Start at *mp*, swell to *mf* in the middle, return to *mp*
- **No accidentals at first** — write in C major or A minor to keep it simple
- Keep it **tonal and consonant** — this week is about sound and register, 
  not harmony

### Notation Tips
- Write the double bass part at sounding pitch — remember it sounds an octave 
  lower than written, so be aware of where your notes will actually land
- Avoid very low positions on the viola (below C3) for now — as Adler notes, 
  each register has its own character, and the viola's lowest positions can 
  sound opaque and indistinct in slow, quiet writing
- Mark bowings clearly: slur pairs of notes for a legato feel, or leave separate 
  for détaché
- Add a tempo marking and dynamic to every part — Forsyth is emphatic that 
  ambiguous dynamics are the first sign of a composer who has not yet heard 
  their own music

## What to Observe & Validate
When the players perform the excerpt, listen and watch for:

1. **Blend** — do the three instruments sound like one voice or three separate 
   ones? Rimsky-Korsakov's claim about string homogeneity is something to test 
   directly here
2. **Balance** — can you hear the cello melody clearly above the bass? Does the 
   viola sit comfortably in the middle?
3. **Register comfort** — do the players look at ease, or are they stretching 
   for awkward positions? This is Adler's point about register and character 
   made physical
4. **Bass weight** — does the double bass feel like Forsyth's "foundation-stone", 
   or does it overpower the voices above?
5. **Dynamic response** — does your written *mp* actually sound like what 
   you imagined?

Ask the players directly after:
- "Does this lie comfortably under your fingers?"
- "Is there anything you would write differently to get this same effect?"
- "Does the low register feel too heavy or just right?"

---

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Writing too low on the viola** — the lowest string (C3) can sound muddy 
   and weak in slow passages. Stay above open G3 for clarity. This is precisely 
   the register character Adler warns about.
2. **Forgetting the double bass transposes** — what you write sounds an octave 
   lower. A written E2 sounds as E1, which may be inaudible or indistinct. 
   Forsyth devotes several pages to this exact trap for student composers.

## Follow-Up Note → Week 2
Once you have heard how these three instruments behave individually in a slow, 
homophonic texture, Week 2 will introduce **simple two-voice independence** — 
giving the cello and viola different rhythmic values, and asking whether the bass 
can sustain a pedal point while the upper voices move. The foundation you validate 
this week directly informs that writing. As Piston puts it, the lower string choir 
is where "the principles of balance, blend, and register are most clearly audible 
and most immediately verifiable" — so everything you notice this week is data 
you carry forward.

## Maestro Discussion Option (if music is not ready)
If the excerpt is not finished in time, bring these questions to the maestro instead:

1. "When writing for low strings together, how do you decide which instrument 
   carries the melody versus the bass line?"
2. "What is the most common mistake you see composers make when writing for 
   double bass?"
3. "How much does register affect blend — for example, is a cello in its upper 
   register harder to balance with a viola?"
4. "Are there any short orchestral passages you would recommend I study 
   specifically for low string writing?"
5. "What dynamic level do low strings naturally sit at in a quiet passage — 
   is *mp* realistic or do they tend to play louder?"
