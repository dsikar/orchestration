# Week 2 — Two-Voice Independence & the Pedal Point

## Relevant Passages from the Literature

**Rimsky-Korsakov — *Principles of Orchestration*, Chapter 1:**
> "The strings form the most important group in the orchestra. They are the most 
> flexible, the most expressive, and the most homogeneous in quality of tone."

He goes on to note that the cello, when given an independent line above a sustained 
bass, creates what he calls the ideal "dual-layer" string texture — the foundation 
of nearly all classical writing for strings alone.

**Walter Piston — *Orchestration*, Chapter 3 (The Strings):**
> "The practice of holding a bass note, or pedal point, while harmonies change above 
> it is one of the most powerful devices available to the orchestrator. The double bass 
> is uniquely suited to this role by virtue of its weight and sustaining quality."

Piston also warns that when cello and viola move in parallel thirds for extended 
passages, they risk losing independence and merging into a single blurred voice — 
something to test directly this week.

---

## Concept Focus
**Two-voice independence between cello and viola, over a held double bass pedal point.**

Building directly on Week 1 (where all three instruments moved together), this week 
you introduce **rhythmic and melodic independence** between the upper two voices, while 
the double bass holds a single sustained note — the pedal — underneath. This is one 
of the most fundamental textures in orchestral writing.

---

## Recommended Player Group
**Double Bass · Cello · Viola**

Same trio as Week 1. This is intentional — you are testing the *same* instruments in 
a more complex relationship, which lets you compare directly against what you heard 
last week.

---

## Deliverable: MUSIC

### The Exercise
Write a passage of **12–16 bars** for viola, cello, and double bass.

### What to Write
- **Tempo**: ♩ = 60–72, moderate and unhurried
- **Time signature**: 4/4
- **Texture**: Two independent melodic voices (viola and cello) over a pedal point 
  (double bass)
- **Content**:
  - **Double bass**: Hold a single open string pedal note for the entire passage 
    (D or G works well — use an open string so the player can sustain without 
    fatigue). One note, whole notes, *pp*.
  - **Cello**: A gentle, stepwise melody in its middle register (D3–G4). 
    Use a mix of quarter notes and half notes. Mostly conjunct motion.
  - **Viola**: A counter-line that moves *against* the cello — when the cello 
    holds, the viola moves; when the viola holds, the cello moves. Aim for 
    contrary or oblique motion rather than parallel movement.
- **Dynamic**: *p* throughout, with one small *cresc/dim* phrase in the middle
- **Key**: D major or D minor — both sit well on these instruments and make use 
  of open strings naturally

### Notation Tips
- Mark each voice clearly — consider adding a brief performance note: 
  "cello and viola independent throughout"
- Avoid writing the viola and cello in the same rhythm for more than two bars 
  consecutively — the whole point is independence
- Resist the urge to fill every beat — rests in one voice while the other moves 
  creates natural breathing and clarity

---

## What to Observe & Validate
1. **Independence** — can you clearly hear two separate melodic ideas, or do they 
   blur into one?
2. **Pedal effect** — does the held bass note create tension and harmonic colour 
   as the harmonies above shift?
3. **Contrary motion** — when viola and cello move in opposite directions, does it 
   feel natural and musical, or forced?
4. **Register separation** — is there enough space between the viola line and the 
   cello line for both to be audible?
5. **Open string resonance** — notice how the double bass open string resonates 
   differently from a stopped note. Does it blend or stick out?

Ask the players:
- "Can you both hear each other's line clearly, or does one tend to dominate?"
- "Does the pedal note feel like a support or does it get in the way harmonically?"
- "Is there a moment where the writing feels unidiomatic — awkward to play?"

---

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Parallel thirds throughout** — as Piston warns, parallel motion between viola 
   and cello for too long collapses the two voices into one. Break it up with 
   contrary motion.
2. **Pedal on a non-open string** — asking the double bass to sustain a stopped 
   note quietly for 16 bars is tiring and intonation becomes unreliable. Stick to 
   open strings (E, A, D, G) for pedal points at this stage.
3. **Too much happening at once** — if your counter-line is too decorated, the 
   texture becomes busy. Simplicity reveals the technique more clearly.

## Follow-Up Note → Week 3
Now that you have tested two-voice independence in the lower strings, Week 3 moves 
upward — introducing the **violin** and exploring how register contrast between 
violin and viola creates the classic four-part string texture. You will also begin 
to think about **octave doubling**, which Rimsky-Korsakov calls the single most 
important tool for strengthening a melodic line in the orchestra.

## Maestro Discussion Option
If the music is not ready, bring these questions to the maestro:

1. "When you see a pedal point in a score, what do you look for in terms of 
   balance between the pedal and the moving voices above?"
2. "How do you coach string players to maintain independence when their lines 
   are rhythmically interlocked?"
3. "Is contrary motion between viola and cello something composers use 
   instinctively, or is it a conscious orchestration decision?"
4. "Can you point me to a passage in our current repertoire where this kind of 
   two-voice string writing is particularly effective?"
