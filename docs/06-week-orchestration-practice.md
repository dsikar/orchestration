# Week 6 — Woodwind Colour Mixing: Flute & Clarinet Together

## Relevant Passages from the Literature

**Cecil Forsyth — *Orchestration*, Chapter 5:**
> "The clarinet is the great chameleon of the woodwind family. In its middle 
> register it blends with strings so completely that the ear cannot separate 
> them. In its upper register it is bright and penetrating. In its lowest 
> register — the chalumeau — it is dark, reedy, and entirely its own creature. 
> No other woodwind instrument offers this range of colour and blend."

Forsyth then makes a crucial observation about the flute-clarinet pairing 
specifically: in their overlapping middle registers, the two instruments blend 
into a third colour that is neither flute nor clarinet — something new, 
hovering and slightly ethereal. He cites this as one of the most useful 
blended timbres in the entire woodwind palette.

**Rimsky-Korsakov — *Principles of Orchestration*, Chapter 2:**
> "The flute is cold and bright in its upper register, hollow and glassy in 
> its middle register, and almost inaudible in its lowest. The clarinet by 
> contrast gains warmth and richness as it descends. This complementary 
> relationship makes them natural partners — where one is weak, the other 
> is strong."

This complementary weakness-and-strength profile is the architectural 
principle behind this week's exercise. You will deliberately place each 
instrument in the register where Rimsky-Korsakov says it is strongest, 
then test what happens when you swap them.

**Walter Piston — *Orchestration*, Chapter 7:**
> "Woodwind doublings at the unison or octave do not merely reinforce — they 
> transform. Two flutes in unison produce a harder, less flexible tone than 
> one. A flute doubled by a clarinet at the unison produces something that is 
> neither flute nor clarinet but a new composite voice with its own character. 
> The orchestrator must learn to think of doublings not as arithmetic — 
> two instruments equals twice the volume — but as chemistry."

Piston's chemistry metaphor is one of the most quoted passages in 
orchestration pedagogy, and it perfectly frames what you are listening 
for this week: not loudness, but transformation of colour.

**Samuel Adler — *The Study of Orchestration*, Chapter 8:**
> "Students learning woodwind scoring for the first time almost always make 
> the same mistake: they write the flute above and the clarinet below, in 
> every passage, in every register, without exception. This is natural — it 
> follows the layout of the score — but it produces a predictable, one-colour 
> result. The real interest begins when the clarinet crosses above the flute, 
> or when both instruments occupy the same register and their timbres begin 
> to compete and interact."

Adler's observation about register crossing is the experimental core of 
this week's second passage.

---

## Concept Focus
**Woodwind colour mixing — how flute and clarinet blend, contrast, and 
transform each other's timbre depending on register, spacing, and which 
instrument carries the melody.**

Having heard a single oboe voice last week, you now introduce two woodwind 
voices simultaneously and explore what Piston calls the chemistry of 
doubling and pairing. The flute and clarinet are chosen because their 
timbral profiles are complementary in exactly the way Rimsky-Korsakov 
describes — and because their interaction produces one of the most 
distinctive and useful colours in the woodwind palette.

---

## Recommended Player Group
**Flute · Clarinet · Viola**

The viola is retained from the string family as a quiet harmonic cushion — 
its middle register blend quality, as Forsyth noted in Week 1, makes it 
the ideal string companion for woodwind writing. It will provide a gentle 
harmonic foundation without competing with either woodwind voice. Ask the 
principal flute and principal clarinet if possible — their instinct for 
blend and balance will be invaluable in the discussion afterward.

## Deliverable: MUSIC

### The Exercise
Write **two passages of 8 bars each** — same melody and harmony, two 
different registral relationships between flute and clarinet.

### Passage A — Natural Position (Flute above, Clarinet below)
- **Flute**: Carries the melody in its warm middle register (A4–E6). 
  Legato, mostly stepwise, quarter and half notes. This is the register 
  Rimsky-Korsakov describes as "hollow and glassy" — present but not 
  brilliant.
- **Clarinet (in Bb)**: A counter-melody or sustained harmony a sixth 
  below the flute, in its richest middle register (written D4–A5, 
  sounding C4–G5). This is where Forsyth says it blends most completely 
  with strings.
- **Viola**: Sustained whole-note harmonies below both woodwinds, *pp*. 
  Root position chords, one per bar. Pure harmonic cushion.
- **Dynamic**: Flute and clarinet both *mp*, viola *pp*

### Passage B — Crossed Position (Clarinet above, Flute below)
- **Clarinet**: Now carries the melody in its bright upper register 
  (written G5–D6, sounding F5–C6). Same melodic material as Passage A 
  but now sitting above the flute.
- **Flute**: Moves to its lower register (C4–G4) — the register 
  Rimsky-Korsakov describes as "almost inaudible." Sustained harmony 
  notes only, half notes, *p*.
- **Viola**: Same sustained harmonies as Passage A, unchanged.
- **Dynamic**: Clarinet *mp*, flute *p*, viola *pp*

### The Comparison
Passage A gives you the standard, expected colour. Passage B gives you 
Adler's "real interest" — the register crossing that produces unexpected 
timbral results. Listen for how the character of the melody changes 
simply by moving it from flute to clarinet, and how the flute in its 
lower register either blends into the texture or struggles to be heard.

### Notation Tips
- Remember that the clarinet in Bb is a transposing instrument — written 
  parts sound a major second lower than notated. If your melody sounds 
  on D, write E in the clarinet part.
- Mark the clarinet part clearly: *"Clarinet in Bb"* at the top of the part
- Keep the flute's lower register notes (Passage B) at *p* or below — 
  Rimsky-Korsakov's warning about inaudibility in that register is real
- Add *dolce* to the flute melody in Passage A — flautists respond 
  immediately to this marking with a rounder, more blended tone
- Write in F major or G major — both sit naturally across all three 
  instruments in these registers

## What to Observe & Validate
1. **The composite colour** — in Passage A, when flute and clarinet play 
   simultaneously in their middle registers, can you identify a third 
   colour that is neither instrument alone? This is Forsyth's ethereal 
   blend.
2. **Chemistry vs arithmetic** — does adding the clarinet to the flute 
   in Passage A simply make it louder, or does it genuinely change the 
   quality of the sound? Test Piston's chemistry metaphor.
3. **Register crossing effect** — in Passage B, does the clarinet in its 
   upper register feel brighter and more focused than the flute melody in 
   Passage A? Does the personality of the melody change?
4. **Flute lower register** — in Passage B, is the flute harmony audible 
   at all, or does it disappear as Rimsky-Korsakov warns? Does this 
   disappearance matter, or does the viola fill the gap?
5. **Viola cushion** — does the sustained viola harmony help the woodwind 
   blend, or does it feel redundant? Remove it mentally and consider 
   whether its absence would change anything.

Ask the players:
- "In Passage A, can you hear each other's line clearly, or do you merge 
  into one voice?"
- "Flautist — in Passage B, does playing in your lower register feel 
  comfortable, or does it feel like the clarinet is covering you?"
- "Clarinettist — does the upper register melody in Passage B feel exposed 
  and soloistic, or does it blend with the flute below?"
- "Does the viola cushion change how you listen to each other, or do you 
  barely notice it?"

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Forgetting the clarinet transposition** — this is the single most 
   common notation error when writing for clarinet for the first time. 
   Double-check every note: if it sounds on C, write D in the Bb clarinet 
   part. Use notation software transposition tools if available, but 
   verify by ear.
2. **Writing the flute too low in Passage B** — below C4 the flute 
   becomes very weak and breathy. In a blended texture this can be a 
   beautiful colour, but in a quiet passage it may simply disappear. 
   Stay at C4 or above.
3. **Overcomplicating the viola part** — the viola this week is a cushion, 
   not a voice. If you give it an interesting inner line, it will distract 
   from the woodwind experiment. Keep it as simple as possible: one note 
   per bar, root position, whole notes.

## Follow-Up Note → Week 7
You have now explored single woodwind voice against strings (Week 5) and 
two woodwind voices in combination (Week 6). Week 7 introduces **brass** 
for the first time — specifically the horn, which Rimsky-Korsakov calls 
"the most orchestral of all instruments" because of its unique ability to 
blend with both strings and woodwinds. As Piston notes, the horn at *pp* 
can add warmth to a string chord without being detected as a separate 
instrument at all — a quality unlike any other brass instrument, and one 
that makes it the natural bridge between the string-woodwind world you 
have been building and the brass family you are about to enter.

## Maestro Discussion Option
If the music is not ready, bring these questions to the maestro:

1. "When you hear flute and clarinet together in a score, what tells you 
   immediately whether the composer understood their blend or was just 
   filling in parts?"
2. "Is the clarinet transposition something that still causes problems 
   in professional scores, or is it second nature by the time a composer 
   is writing for orchestra?"
3. "Rimsky-Korsakov warns that the flute's lower register is almost 
   inaudible — do you find that to be true in our hall, or is it more 
   nuanced than that?"
4. "How do you coach woodwind players to blend when their natural 
   instinct is to project as soloists?"
5. "Can you point me to a passage in our current repertoire where the 
   flute-clarinet combination is used in a way that particularly 
   surprised or impressed you?"
