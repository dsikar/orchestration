# Week 3 — Register Contrast & Octave Doubling: Introducing the Violin

## Relevant Passages from the Literature

**Rimsky-Korsakov — *Principles of Orchestration*, Chapter 1:**
> "Doubling in the octave is the most natural and the most frequently used method 
> of strengthening a melodic line. A melody given to violins in the upper octave, 
> with cellos doubling it an octave below, acquires a fullness and richness of tone 
> that neither instrument can achieve alone."

He distinguishes carefully between doubling for *strength* (same note, same rhythm, 
different octave) and doubling for *colour* (same note, different timbre, same octave) 
— this week focuses on the former.

**Walter Piston — *Orchestration*, Chapter 3:**
> "The four-part string texture — first violin, second violin, viola, cello — is the 
> bedrock of orchestral writing. The composer who has not thoroughly explored what 
> these four voices can do in every combination has not yet begun to understand 
> the orchestra."

Piston recommends that beginners write for strings alone for as long as possible 
before introducing winds, precisely because the string family exposes every weakness 
in voice-leading and balance with nowhere to hide.

**Samuel Adler — *The Study of Orchestration*, Chapter 2:**
> "Register is not merely a matter of pitch — it is a matter of character. The violin 
> in its lowest register (G string) has a dark, intense quality entirely unlike its 
> brilliant upper register. The composer must know not just the range of an instrument 
> but the personality of each register within that range."

---

## Concept Focus
**Register contrast across the full string choir, and the use of octave doubling 
to reinforce a melodic line.**

This is the first week you introduce the **violin**. With violin added to your viola, 
cello, and double bass, you now have access to the complete tonal spectrum of the 
string family — from the brilliant upper register of the violin down to the deep 
resonance of the bass. This week you explore how spreading voices across registers 
creates clarity, and how doubling a melody at the octave transforms its weight 
and presence.

---

## Recommended Player Group
**First Violin · Viola · Cello**

Leave the double bass out this week — three players is your limit during the break, 
and removing the bass forces you to hear the middle and upper string voices with 
full attention. You can ask the principal first violin, or any desk violin player.

## Deliverable: MUSIC

### The Exercise
Write **two short passages of 8 bars each** — same melody, two different 
orchestrations — so you can compare them back to back.

### Passage A — Melody with Harmony
- **Violin**: A simple, singable 8-bar melody in its middle register (D4–A5). 
  Mostly stepwise, one or two leaps of a third or fourth. Quarter and half notes.
- **Viola**: A sustained inner harmony — a third or sixth below the violin, 
  moving only when the violin moves. Whole notes and half notes only.
- **Cello**: The bass line — root-position notes of the implied harmony, 
  one note per bar. Half notes.
- **Dynamic**: *mp* throughout

### Passage B — Octave Doubling
- **Violin**: Exactly the same melody as Passage A, unchanged.
- **Viola**: Now doubles the violin melody **one octave below** — same rhythm, 
  same notes, one octave down. No inner harmony.
- **Cello**: Same bass line as Passage A.
- **Dynamic**: *mp* throughout — same as Passage A

### The Comparison
By keeping everything identical except the viola part, you isolate the single 
variable: octave doubling versus inner harmony. Listen carefully to what changes 
in weight, colour, and projection.

### Notation Tips
- Mark the two passages clearly: **"Passage A"** and **"Passage B"** in the score
- Add a brief note to the players: *"Please play both passages at the same dynamic 
  — I am comparing the texture, not the volume"*
- Keep the melody simple enough that it is instantly recognisable through both 
  orchestrations
- Write in G major or E minor — both sit naturally on all three instruments

---

## What to Observe & Validate
1. **Projection** — does the melody feel stronger and more present in Passage B 
   (octave doubling) than in Passage A?
2. **Colour change** — does the viola doubling at the octave below add darkness 
   and weight, or does it muddy the line?
3. **Loss of inner harmony** — in Passage B, without the viola filling in harmony, 
   does the texture feel thin or lean and focused?
4. **G string character** — if the viola doubling brings the line down to its G or 
   C string, notice the tonal change — darker, more intense, as Adler describes
5. **Blend** — do violin and viola in octaves sound like one instrument or two?

Ask the players:
- "Which passage feels more natural to play — the harmony version or the doubling?"
- "In Passage B, does the viola line sit comfortably in register, or does it feel 
  like it is being pulled away from its natural voice?"
- "Can you describe the difference in how the two passages feel from the inside 
  of the ensemble?"

---

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Melody too wide in range** — if your 8-bar melody spans more than a tenth, 
   the viola doubling an octave below may drop into an uncomfortably low register. 
   Keep the melody's range narrow this week (within an octave is ideal).
2. **Doubling at the unison instead of the octave** — doubling at the same pitch 
   simply reinforces volume; doubling at the octave changes colour and weight. Make 
   sure you are writing a true octave displacement.
3. **Forgetting that viola reads alto clef** — if you are writing by hand or in 
   notation software, double-check that the viola part is in the correct clef. 
   A common beginner error is accidentally writing viola in treble clef throughout.

## Follow-Up Note → Week 4
You have now heard melody, inner harmony, and octave doubling across three string 
voices. Week 4 introduces **the second violin** to complete the four-part string 
texture, and explores **divisi writing** — splitting a single string section into 
two independent voices. This is where Piston's "bedrock" four-part texture becomes 
fully available to you.

## Maestro Discussion Option
If the music is not ready, bring these questions to the maestro:

1. "When you look at a string passage that uses octave doubling, what tells you 
   whether the composer has used it for strength or for colour?"
2. "Is there a register on the violin or viola that you feel is underused by 
   composers — a character that does not appear often enough in scores?"
3. "How do you balance a doubled octave line against an inner harmony — is it 
   purely dynamic, or does articulation play a role?"
4. "Could you point me to a passage in our repertoire where the string writing 
   uses register contrast particularly effectively?"
5. "At what point does octave doubling become too thick — when does it start to 
   work against clarity rather than for it?"
