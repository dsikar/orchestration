# Week 5 — Dynamics & Balance: The First Woodwind Voice

## Relevant Passages from the Literature

**Walter Piston — *Orchestration*, Chapter 6:**
> "The oboe is the most penetrating of the woodwind instruments at all dynamic 
> levels. A single oboe playing at *mf* will be heard clearly above a string 
> section of twenty players at the same dynamic marking. The orchestrator must 
> learn to think not in terms of written dynamics alone, but in terms of the 
> actual acoustic weight each instrument carries in the room."

Piston uses this observation to introduce what he calls the fundamental 
asymmetry of the orchestra — that instruments are not equal in projection, 
and that the composer's job is to manage this inequality consciously rather 
than pretend it does not exist.

**Rimsky-Korsakov — *Principles of Orchestration*, Chapter 2:**
> "The woodwind instruments, taken as a group, are soloists by nature. Unlike 
> the strings, which blend into a mass, each woodwind voice retains its 
> individual character at almost every dynamic level. This is their great 
> strength and their great danger — they stand out whether the composer 
> intends them to or not."

He follows this with a practical table showing the relative penetrating power 
of each woodwind instrument, ranking the oboe and piccolo as the most cutting, 
the flute in its lower register and the bass clarinet as the least. This week 
you will test the oboe end of that spectrum directly.

**Samuel Adler — *The Study of Orchestration*, Chapter 7:**
> "When introducing a woodwind instrument into a string texture for the first 
> time, the student composer invariably writes the wind too loud. The strings 
> mask the wind at forte; the wind obliterates the strings at piano. Finding 
> the dynamic window in which both families speak clearly and in balance is 
> the central practical skill of orchestration."

Adler recommends that students write their first woodwind-plus-strings passage 
at *p* for the strings and *pp* for the wind, then adjust upward from there 
based on what they hear — exactly the approach this week's exercise takes.

**Cecil Forsyth — *Orchestration*, Chapter 5:**
> "The clarinet is the great chameleon of the woodwind family. In its middle 
> register it blends with strings so completely that the ear cannot separate 
> them. In its upper register it is bright and penetrating. In its lowest 
> register — the chalumeau — it is dark, reedy, and entirely its own creature. 
> No other woodwind instrument offers this range of colour and blend."

Forsyth's description of the clarinet's chalumeau register is one of the most 
celebrated passages in orchestration literature, and sets up a direct comparison 
with the oboe that you can test this week.

---

## Concept Focus
**Dynamic balance between a single woodwind voice and the string choir — 
understanding acoustic weight, projection, and the dynamic window in which 
both families coexist clearly.**

This is the first week you step outside the string family entirely. Everything 
you have built over the past four weeks — register awareness, voice independence, 
four-part texture — now serves as the backdrop against which a single woodwind 
voice enters. The central question this week is simple but profound: at what 
dynamic level does the woodwind blend with the strings, and at what level does 
it dominate or disappear?

---

## Recommended Player Group
**Oboe · Violin · Cello**

The oboe is chosen deliberately as your first woodwind encounter because, as 
Piston and Rimsky-Korsakov both note, it is the most projecting and the least 
forgiving of dynamic miscalculation. If you can balance an oboe against strings, 
you can balance anything. The violin and cello give you a two-octave string 
frame — treble and bass — within which the oboe's middle voice will either 
sit naturally or expose itself. Ask the principal oboist if possible — they 
will have the most nuanced sense of their instrument's projection.

---

## Deliverable: MUSIC

### The Exercise
Write **three short versions of the same 8-bar passage** — identical notes, 
three different dynamic relationships between the oboe and strings. This is 
a controlled experiment in acoustic balance.

### The Passage (all three versions)
- **Oboe**: A simple, lyrical melody of 8 bars in its middle register (G4–D6). 
  Stepwise motion, mostly quarter and half notes. No large leaps. 
  This is the same melody in all three versions.
- **Violin**: A sustained inner harmony — a sixth above the oboe line where 
  possible, moving only when necessary. Whole notes and half notes.
- **Cello**: A simple bass line, root-position harmonies, one note per bar. 
  Half notes. Arco.

### Version 1 — Equal Dynamics
- All three instruments: *mp* throughout
- Listen for: does the oboe dominate, blend, or disappear?

### Version 2 — Adler's Recommendation
- **Oboe**: *pp*
- **Strings**: *p*
- Listen for: does the balance shift? Is the oboe still audible? Does it 
  blend more naturally into the string texture?

### Version 3 — Strings Recede
- **Oboe**: *mf*
- **Strings**: *pp*
- Listen for: does the oboe now function as a clear soloist above an 
  accompanying string background?

### Notation Tips
- Mark the dynamic of each version clearly at the start of each passage — 
  do not rely on the players to remember verbal instructions
- Add *espress.* to the oboe line if you want a singing, forward tone — 
  oboists respond well to expressive markings
- Keep the cello bass line simple enough that the cellist can focus on 
  listening and adjusting rather than reading
- Write in F major or D minor — both sit naturally in the oboe's most 
  resonant middle register

---

## What to Observe & Validate
1. **Projection asymmetry** — in Version 1, does the oboe at *mp* already 
   feel louder than the strings at *mp*? This is Piston's fundamental 
   asymmetry made audible.
2. **The blend window** — in Version 2, at what moment does the oboe stop 
   sounding like a soloist and start sounding like part of the texture? 
   Is Adler's *pp/p* recommendation borne out in the room?
3. **Soloist clarity** — in Version 3, does *mf* oboe over *pp* strings 
   feel like an accompanied melody, or does it feel unbalanced and exposed?
4. **Register sensitivity** — if the oboe melody dips into its lower 
   register (below E4), does the balance shift? Lower oboe notes project 
   less than upper ones.
5. **Room acoustics** — remember you are in a rehearsal room, not a concert 
   hall. The balance you hear this week may differ in performance. Ask the 
   players whether they think the hall would change the equation.

Ask the players:
- "As an oboist, which dynamic version felt most natural and comfortable 
  to you as a solo voice?"
- "Violinist — in which version could you hear the oboe most clearly from 
  inside the ensemble?"
- "Cellist — did the bass line feel like it was supporting the oboe, or 
  competing with it in any of the versions?"
- "If you were performing this in a concert hall rather than a rehearsal 
  room, which version do you think would balance best?"

---

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Writing the oboe too low** — the oboe below D4 becomes thin and 
   effortful. In a quiet string texture this can make the oboe sound 
   strained rather than expressive. Keep the melody above E4 this week.
2. **Ignoring breath** — unlike string players, the oboist must breathe. 
   An 8-bar legato line with no rests may be playable but uncomfortable. 
   Build in at least one natural breath point — a quarter rest or a phrase 
   break — within the melody.
3. **Assuming equal dynamics mean equal volume** — as Rimsky-Korsakov's 
   table makes clear, a woodwind instrument and a string instrument at the 
   same written dynamic are not the same acoustic volume. Version 1 exists 
   precisely to demonstrate this assumption's failure.

## Follow-Up Note → Week 6
You have now heard what happens when a single woodwind voice enters a string 
texture and how dynamic balance must be actively managed rather than assumed. 
Week 6 extends this to **two woodwind voices together** — specifically flute 
and clarinet — and introduces the question of woodwind blend and colour mixing. 
As Forsyth's description of the clarinet's registers suggests, the clarinet is 
the ideal second woodwind to introduce because its chameleon quality means it 
can either blend with the flute or contrast with it depending on register — 
giving you two completely different colours from a single instrument choice.

## Maestro Discussion Option
If the music is not ready, bring these questions to the maestro:

1. "How do you manage the balance between a solo woodwind and the string 
   section in rehearsal — is it primarily a dynamic adjustment or do you 
   also change articulation and bowing?"
2. "Is there a woodwind instrument you find consistently hardest to balance 
   against strings, and why?"
3. "When Rimsky-Korsakov talks about woodwinds being soloists by nature, 
   do you think that is still true in a modern concert hall with a large 
   string section?"
4. "How does the acoustic of our specific hall affect woodwind-string balance 
   compared to what a composer might imagine at the piano?"
5. "Can you point me to a passage in our current repertoire where a single 
   woodwind voice is balanced against strings in a way you find particularly 
   well-judged?"
